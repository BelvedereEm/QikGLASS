How the head works

The printed body drops into the spindle clamp where the 775 motor sits. An 8 mm steel shaft runs through two LM8UU linear bearings, so it can slide up and down and spin freely. A compression spring under the body pushes the shaft down. That spring, not the Z motor, sets the scoring pressure, so small differences in bed or glass height barely change the force.

The QWORK cutter head is unscrewed from its handle and clamped into the carrier at the bottom of the shaft, 1.5 mm off-centre. The wheel trails behind the pivot like a shopping-cart caster and steers itself around curves. The G-code generator shifts every path by that offset so the wheel lands on your line.

Measure two things first

I couldn't confirm either size from the product listings. Measure both with calipers, enter them at the top of qikglass_scoring_head.scad, then export the STLs from free OpenSCAD. The included STLs use the defaults shown.

Clamp bore

Take the spindle out and measure the motor body where the clamp grips it. A bare 775 is about 42 mm. If the clamp grips the steel flux ring, it's about 45.5 mm.

clamp_d = 42.0

Cutter-head shank

Loosen the small screw on the handle (the kit's screwdriver fits), pull a head out, and measure the round part that sat inside the handle.

head_shank_d = 7.0

Assembly

  1. Press both LM8UU bearings into the body from the top until they sit on the bottom lip.
  2. Screw the cap onto the body with the three countersunk screws. The cap holds the bearings in and its flange rests on top of the spindle clamp.
  3. Slide the shaft collar onto the shaft, then push the shaft down through the cap and both bearings.
  4. From below, slide the spring over the shaft, then push the carrier on until the shaft bottoms in its socket. Tighten the side screw.
  5. Pull the shaft down to compress the spring a few millimetres, then lock the collar against the cap. That sets the preload. More preload gives a steadier force.
  6. Push the cutter head into the carrier's bottom pocket. Turn it so the wheel's plane lines up with the two grooves on the carrier's sides, then tighten the screw. If the wheel is turned sideways, it won't steer.
  7. Spin the shaft by hand. It should turn freely and spring back when you push it up.

Swapping spindle ⇄ glass head

  1. Power down and unplug the spindle lead at the board.
  2. Loosen the clamp, lift the spindle out, drop the head in until the cap flange rests on the clamp, and tighten.
  3. Re-zero Z on the glass every time (see Calibrate & score).
  4. Going back: reverse this, plug the spindle in, and re-zero Z with a bit.

Never run a milling file with the glass head fitted. Router files dive several millimetres deep and will crush the spring and break glass. Keep glass files in their own folder on the SD card.

Put every blank in the same place

The G-code always puts X0 Y0 at the blank's front-left corner, with the 6 in side running along X. Two things keep that repeatable: a jig that doesn't move, and a quick zeroing routine each time the machine is switched on.

Fit the jig once

  1. Screw a flat MDF spoilboard to the bed and glue 2 mm felt or cork where the glass will sit. Set felt_t in the CAD file to your felt thickness. The jig rails then stand 1.5 mm above the felt, which is high enough to stop the glass but lower than 3 mm glass, so the wheel never touches them.
  2. Print qikglass_jig.stl. It's an L-shaped corner stop for a 6 × 4 blank lying flat, with its long rail along X. The rail continues to the right and has a small stop for a 30 × 20 mm scrap of the same glass, which is the wheel-alignment pad. The pad's centre is marked, and it sits at X179.4 Y10, the page's default Pad X and Pad Y.
  3. Place the jig at the front-left of the work area, leaving at least 20 mm of travel below X0 and Y0.
  4. Square it to the machine: fit the zero pointer (below), lower it just above the front rail's inside edge, and jog along X. Tap the jig until the tip follows the edge along its whole length, then screw it down with small countersunk wood screws.

Zero X and Y

The 3018 doesn't know where it is when it's switched on, so X0 Y0 has to be set at the jig corner. There are two ways to do it.

  1. Zero pointer (any time, most accurate): loosen the carrier's screw, slide the carrier off the shaft and push on qikglass_pointer.stl. Its tip sits exactly on the pivot axis, which is the point the G-code moves. Jog the tip into the jig's inside corner, just above the felt, and press the controller's XY zero (or send G10 L20 P1 X0 Y0). Swap the carrier back on and zero Z on the glass as usual.
  2. Park and power off (quick): every QikGLASS file ends with the head parked at X0 Y0. Switch the machine off there, don't move the gantry by hand, and after switching on just press XY zero. Check it with the pointer every few sessions, or after anything bumps the machine.

If your 3018 has limit switches, you can turn on homing ($22=1) and save the jig corner as a permanent work offset with G10 L2 P1. Then $H at power-up restores it with no touching off. This is optional. The routine above works without switches.

Every blank

  • Brush the felt clean, then push the blank firmly into the jig corner, 6 in side along the front rail. The corner notch leaves room for a chipped corner.
  • Keep the scrap pad strip against its stop. Replace it when it's covered in scratches.
  • Any rectangle works in the jig, including the leftover strip from an earlier job. Set Glass blank to “Custom size” and type its width (along the front rail) and depth in inches or mm. After a job that saves a strip, “Use as next blank” fills the size in for you, and the page remembers it until next time. Glass wider than 160 mm hits the pad stop.
  • Zero Z on the glass surface (paper pinch), then run the file.

Set the pressure with a kitchen scale

  1. Put the scale on the bed under the wheel and tare it.
  2. Jog Z down in 0.1 mm steps until the scale first jumps (that's the spring preload). Call that height "touch".
  3. Keep stepping down until the scale reads about 3 kg. The distance below "touch" is your Score depth.
  4. Run a test on scrap. A good score is a thin, even, barely visible line that makes a steady hiss. White flakes or a gritty crunch mean too much pressure. Skips or a line that won't break mean too little. Adjust in 0.5 kg steps. Most stained glass lands at 3–5 kg.

The 3018's Z motor can push well past this, but its frame flexes. If lines wander under load, add spring preload rather than more depth.

Zero Z on the glass for every sheet

Lay a strip of paper on the glass under the wheel and jog down until the paper is just pinched. Set Z0 there. The program then drives to Z −(score depth) and the spring supplies the force.

Draw scores the way glass breaks

  • Draw each score as one open line in Inkscape (document in mm, page = your sheet). Draw only score lines. Don't draw the sheet outline.
  • Each score runs from edge to edge, or ends where it meets an earlier score. Glass can't break along a line that stops in the middle of the sheet.
  • Never cross or retrace a score. The generator warns you if two lines cross.
  • Keep curves gentle. Deep inside curves need extra relief scores, as with hand cutting.
  • Break in the numbered order shown in the preview, running each score before the next one that ends on it.

Pieces, 6 × 4 blanks and relief scores

  • Every job goes on a 6 × 4 in blank (152.4 × 101.6 mm). Push the blank into the corner fence so its front-left corner is X0 Y0.
  • With Layout set to “Pack bottom-left”, pieces fill a row from left to right starting at the front-left corner, then the next row up, with a gap between them. Pieces are turned 90° when that keeps the rows low. Turn this off for streaky glass with a grain. Any pieces that don't fit move onto blank 2, 3, and so on, each with its own G-code file.
  • The unused glass ends up as one strip along the back. The trim score T runs across the blank above the top row: break it first and keep the strip for another project. Relief scores stop at the trim line, so the strip stays clean.
  • A closed outline is treated as a piece, with everything outside it as waste. With Relief scores set to Auto, the page adds relief lines out to the glass edge: a pinwheel of tangent lines around circles and outside curves, extensions of each sharp outside corner, and a comb of parallel lines out of inside curves such as a semicircle bite.
  • Relief lines (purple, marked R) are scored after the outline and end on it without crossing it. Break them first, starting with the ones nearest the glass edge, using running pliers or grozers. Then run the piece outline.
  • Sharp corners are split into separate sides, because a swivel wheel can't turn on the spot. Sharp inside corners are flagged. Score up to them from both sides and finish with a grinder.
  • Cut neighbouring pieces from separate blanks. Two outlines a line-width apart leave a sliver that won't break, and the page warns you when that happens.

Oil and running

  • The head has no reservoir once it's out of the handle. Touch a drop of cutting oil to the wheel before each sheet, or brush a thin film along the lines. Use the "Pause to re-oil" option for big sheets. It uses M0. If your offline controller ignores M0, run from a PC with Candle or UGS instead.
  • Files contain M5 and never turn the spindle on, but keep it unplugged anyway.
  • Wear safety glasses. Handle sheets with cut-resistant gloves, and brush chips off the felt between sheets so the glass sits flat.
